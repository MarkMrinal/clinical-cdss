CLINICAL CDSS REPLICATION
PRESENTATION QUESTIONS & ANSWERS
============================================================

Purpose of this guide
---------------------
This document explains what the prototype actually does, how it relates to the
base paper, and where our implementation differs. Use the “What we can say”
sentences during the presentation. They are deliberately precise: this is a
research and education prototype, not a clinically validated decision system.


THE SHORT VERSION — ABOUT 30 SECONDS
-----------------------------------
“A free-text case is first structured by a Clarifier role. The system then uses
a BioBERT-based embedding model to find relevant passages in a local Qdrant
index, asks an LLM role to form evidence-linked candidate hypotheses, searches
PubMed for related abstracts, and compares the two evidence streams. A
transparent project-specific confidence heuristic determines whether the
optimizer asks the retrieval stages to try a refined query, up to three cycles.
Python coordinates the stages; the same Gemini model is called separately for
the LLM tasks. This follows the paper’s five-role architecture, but it is our
own implementation and is not clinically validated.”


1. WHY DOES THE PAGE SHOW “SHOW ORIGINAL BENCHMARK ANSWER CHOICES”?
-------------------------------------------------------------------
Those choices belong to saved exam questions, not to a live patient chat. A
benchmark is a fixed collection of questions and known answers used to compare
systems consistently. Our project has frozen samples from:

  • MedQA: US medical licensing-style multiple-choice questions.
  • PubMedQA: questions answered using biomedical research abstracts; its
    common form asks for yes/no/maybe and a supporting answer.
  • MedBullets: medical exam questions, including multiple-choice items in the
    sample we prepared.

We froze a seeded sample (seed 42) and kept its question IDs so baseline,
RAG-only, and full-pipeline runs use the same items. “Frozen” means the
evaluation sample is fixed and reproducible; it does not mean live users must
choose from those questions. The original answer choices are shown only when
replaying a benchmark example, so the audience can see the exam question as it
was posed. The live analysis box accepts free text and does not require options.

Our completed evaluation was 20 MedQA questions: baseline 17/20 (85%), RAG-only
17/20 (85%), full pipeline 18/20 (90%). PubMedQA and MedBullets were prepared
but not evaluated. With only 20 items, the one-answer difference is preliminary
and does not establish statistical superiority.

What we can say:
“The collapsed choices are part of the saved MedQA benchmark record. They are
there for transparency when replaying an exam case; the live demo is free text.”


2. WHAT HAPPENS IN EACH ITERATION? WHAT ARE THE COUNTS?
------------------------------------------------------
One full iteration has these steps:

  1) The RAG Analyzer embeds the current retrieval query and searches the local
     Qdrant collections for relevant text passages.
  2) The LLM is asked to suggest candidate conditions/hypotheses grounded in
     those passages. Unsupported candidates are intended to be discarded.
  3) The Evidence Scanner searches PubMed for abstracts relevant to the
     candidate hypotheses and the structured case.
  4) The Fusion role compares the local passages and PubMed records, describes
     agreement, conflicts, and evidence gaps, and calculates the project’s
     confidence heuristic.
  5) If confidence is below 0.65 and another cycle is allowed, the Optimizer
     proposes a more focused query. The next cycle repeats retrieval, candidate
     generation, PubMed search, and fusion using that query/focus.

The Clarifier runs once before the loop. It creates the initial structured
case and retrieval query. The optimizer updates the query and focus; it does
not ask the user to re-enter the case.

“RAG passages” are snippets of source text retrieved from our local index. RAG
means retrieval-augmented generation: retrieve relevant material, then give it
to a language model as context. The passages are not themselves diagnoses.

“Supported hypotheses” are candidate conditions the RAG Analyzer produced and
linked to retrieved passages. “Supported” means supported by that retrieval
stage according to the program’s prompt and checks; it does not mean confirmed
or clinically proven. The Fusion stage separately checks whether PubMed results
also appear to support candidates.

“PubMed records” are literature records fetched from NCBI PubMed, including
title, abstract, publication details, PMID, and URL when available. They are
research references, not necessarily guidelines or evidence that applies to a
particular patient.

The interface’s count is the number of items returned for that stage in that
iteration. It does not represent a probability or a quality rating. Depending
on the view/log, a displayed count can be for retrieved passages, hypotheses,
or PubMed records; describe the label shown rather than treating these counts
as interchangeable.


3. WHERE DOES THE CLARIFIER AGENT APPEAR?
----------------------------------------
The Clarifier converts the original free text into structured fields. In the
result page, “Reported symptoms” mainly displays the symptoms extracted from
that structure. “Key concepts” displays search concepts intended to guide
retrieval. Other fields can include duration, severity, medications, tests,
age/sex if supplied, missing information, and the retrieval query.

The Clarifier is not a separate visible chat bubble. Its output is shown in the
structured summary and informs the downstream search. In this implementation,
the LLM extracts and organizes text; it does not perform the paper’s claimed
SNOMED CT/ICD-10 coding. That coding is not implemented here.


4. WHAT IS THE CONFIDENCE SCORE? WHAT ARE THE SCORES BESIDE RAG PASSAGES?
------------------------------------------------------------------------
The overall confidence number is a project-specific heuristic from 0 to 1. It
is used as a control signal for whether to try another retrieval cycle. It is
not a clinically calibrated probability, and 0.83 does not mean “83% chance
the answer is correct.”

Our code computes:

  confidence = 0.35 × retrieval_similarity
             + 0.25 × evidence_breadth
             + 0.20 × web_support
             + 0.20 × cross_source_agreement

Where:
  • retrieval_similarity is the mean similarity score of retrieved passages.
  • evidence_breadth is retrieved passage count divided by top-k (5), capped
    at 1.0.
  • web_support is the fraction of candidate hypotheses whose condition words
    overlap sufficiently with a PubMed title/abstract under a simple token
    matching rule.
  • cross_source_agreement is the fraction of RAG candidate conditions that
    also appear among those PubMed-supported candidates.

The weights are our replication heuristic. The paper describes a confidence
threshold and relevant evidence factors, but does not publish this formula or
these weights. We have not validated or calibrated the score against clinical
outcomes. The threshold in this project is 0.65.

The small “Score” beside each RAG passage is a retrieval similarity score: how
close the passage embedding is to the query embedding in vector space. It is
not the overall confidence and does not mean that passage is that likely to be
true. Higher is closer under this embedding/search setup, not necessarily more
clinically reliable.

What we can say:
“The overall score is an auditable research heuristic used to trigger another
search cycle. It is not a probability or a measure validated for patient care.”


5. WHAT DOES “HEURISTIC SCORE” MEAN? HOW DO WE KNOW IT IS VALID?
--------------------------------------------------------------
A heuristic is a practical rule built from selected signals. We combine
retrieval similarity, how many passages were found, simple PubMed text support,
and overlap between the two evidence streams. The implementation uses the
explicit weighted formula above, and logs its components so we can inspect why
the final value was produced.

We do not know that this score is clinically valid. We have not calibrated it
against a sufficiently large labeled dataset, measured reliability across
clinical cases, or validated it with clinicians. The 0.65 threshold follows
the paper’s reported threshold, but our score formula is not the paper’s
published formula—the paper does not give its weights/formula. Therefore the
paper’s reported threshold and our heuristic are not guaranteed to have the
same meaning or calibration.

The benchmark accuracy is a separate evaluation. It does not validate the
confidence score. To validate confidence, future work would need a larger
representative labeled set, calibration analysis (for example, whether cases
scored near 0.8 are actually correct about 80% of the time), and expert review.


6. WHY DOES THE OUTPUT SAY “RAG CANDIDATE — NOT A DIAGNOSIS”?
------------------------------------------------------------
“Epistaxis” is the medical term for a nosebleed. “Epistaxis associated with
mucosal bleeding” is a candidate concept surfaced by retrieval in the shown
case. It describes the symptom/evidence topic; it does not identify why the
nosebleed happened or establish a disease.

The generated response is the system’s text answer. For the live example, it
said the combination of high fever, vomiting, and bleeding warrants prompt
medical evaluation. That is a safety-oriented generated response, not a
confirmed diagnosis. The two areas answer different questions:

  • RAG candidate: what concept/condition-like phrase appeared in the retrieved
    evidence stage?
  • Generated response: what short response did the final language-model
    stage produce from the case and evidence report?

The system does not establish a cause from the supplied text. The disclaimer is
important because this prototype is not validated for patient care. Do not
present the candidate phrase as the person’s diagnosis.


7. WHY DID DEMO 2 RUN TWICE? HOW MANY CYCLES ARE ALLOWED? DOES THIS MATCH THE PAPER?
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The full pipeline compares its heuristic confidence with threshold 0.65 after
each fusion step. When the score is below 0.65 and the maximum has not been
reached, the Optimizer produces feedback and a refined retrieval query. If the
score meets/exceeds 0.65, the loop stops. If confidence remains low at the
maximum, it stops because of the iteration limit and returns the available
result with that limitation recorded.

The project maximum is three iterations/cycles (`MAX_ITERS=3` by default). A
case can stop after one, two, or three; three is a maximum, not a promise to
always run three. In the saved adaptive demonstration, cycle 1 scored below
threshold, prompting optimizer feedback; cycle 2 scored above threshold, so it
stopped. The normal saved demonstration met the threshold on its first cycle.

The paper reports a confidence threshold of 0.65 and a maximum of three
optimization cycles. Those controls are mirrored in our configuration. The
implementation is still a simplification: we use our own unpublished-by-paper
heuristic, explicit Python orchestration rather than CrewAI, and different
search/model details. Thus we follow the high-level stopping design, not a
bit-for-bit reproduction of the paper.


8. WHAT DATABASES AND MODELS DO WE USE? ARE THEY THE SAME AS THE PAPER?
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
There are two local Qdrant collections used for vector retrieval:

  1) Biomedical collection: about 2,800 PubMed abstracts split into about
     11,244 text chunks.
  2) Textbooks collection: a balanced sample from 18 books in the public
     MedRAG textbook corpus, up to 780 passages per book; 13,691 passages were
     indexed after filtering.

The index was built locally. Qdrant is the vector database engine; it stores
passage text/metadata and embedding vectors. It is not itself a medical source.
PubMed is maintained by the US National Library of Medicine and is an official
biomedical citation/abstract database. Our textbook passages come from the
public MedRAG research corpus; that does not make the set an official clinical
guideline collection or a hospital-approved knowledge base. We did not claim to
copy a named book list from the paper: its text did not provide a usable exact
list for our replication, so we chose the public MedRAG corpus and documented
that choice.

The embedding model ID configured in this project is
`pritamdeka/BioBERT-mnli-snli-scinli-scitail-mednli-stsb`. It is a BioBERT-based
SentenceTransformer model tuned for biomedical/clinical text similarity and
inference tasks. It encodes a query and passages into vectors so Qdrant can
retrieve semantically related text. It does not generate the final answer.
README records this model ID as matching the model named in the paper. The
paper’s broader corpus and web-search setup are not identical to ours.

Our live external search is PubMed via NCBI E-utilities. The paper describes a
broader web-intelligence setup, including sources/services beyond PubMed. Our
local corpus, source mix, and implementation are therefore partial
replications, not the paper’s exact database environment.

“Official database” and “same database as the paper” are different claims:
PubMed is an official NLM service, but our selected PubMed records and MedRAG
passages are not necessarily the same sources used for the paper’s results.


9. WHAT HAPPENS WHEN SOMEONE TYPES TEXT AND CLICKS ANALYZE?
---------------------------------------------------------
In the live page, the web server passes the free-text input to the existing
Python pipeline. The stages are:

  INPUT TEXT
     ↓
  Clarifier — Gemini structures symptoms, duration, missing details, concepts,
              and an initial retrieval query.
     ↓
  Local retrieval — BioBERT-based encoder creates vectors; Qdrant searches the
                    biomedical and textbook collections.
     ↓
  RAG Analyzer — Gemini receives the case and retrieved passages and returns
                 candidate hypotheses with passage-grounded rationale.
     ↓
  Evidence Scanner — Python queries NCBI PubMed; results include abstracts and
                     publication metadata. Gemini may help form search terms,
                     depending on configuration.
     ↓
  Fusion — Gemini summarizes agreement/conflict; Python calculates the
           weighted heuristic score.
     ↓
  Optimizer — if score < 0.65 and cycle limit not reached, Gemini proposes a
              refined query; Python repeats retrieval/evidence/fusion.
     ↓
  Final selection — Gemini generates the final free-text response (or selects
                    an option for benchmark questions); Python saves the audit
                    log, and the page displays the output and evidence.

The page and Python server do not train a model during analysis. The embedding
model and local index already exist. The system performs retrieval, external
requests, several LLM requests, deterministic score calculations, and logging.
Live response time depends on network/API availability and the number of
iterations. Saved demonstrations replay local logs and do not make live API
calls; a live free-text analysis does.


10. IS THIS REALLY FIVE AGENTS IF THEY ALL USE GEMINI?
----------------------------------------------------
The honest answer is: there are five distinct agent roles in the software
workflow, but they are not five independent AI models or five autonomous
services. The Clarifier, RAG Analyzer, Evidence Scanner, Fusion, and Optimizer
have different prompts, inputs, outputs, and responsibilities. The Python
pipeline calls the same configured Gemini model separately for those LLM
tasks. The API does not keep one continuous conversation that personally
performs every stage; our Python program controls the order and passes each
stage the relevant data. The roles may be separate API calls, but they share a
model/provider and are not independent model systems.

Some work is not done by Gemini at all:
  • Python structures requests, controls the loop, checks stop conditions,
    formats results, and writes logs.
  • The embedding model encodes queries and passages.
  • Qdrant performs vector search.
  • NCBI E-utilities retrieves PubMed records.
  • Python labels evidence levels and calculates the confidence formula.
  • The browser displays the result.

The paper used multiple LLMs for different roles and CrewAI orchestration. We
use one Gemini model for the five prompt-defined roles and explicit Python
orchestration. This is a clear deviation. It is reasonable for a student
prototype, but it means we should not claim five independent AI models or
equivalent autonomy to the paper.

If asked “isn’t it just one model?” say:
“It is one underlying Gemini model assigned to five specialized prompt roles,
with each stage invoked separately and orchestrated by our Python pipeline.
The retrieval, PubMed access, scoring, and control flow are separate software
components. We acknowledge that this is less independent than using five
different models; model diversity is a limitation and a future improvement.”


HOW TO EXPLAIN THE TWO SAVED DEMONSTRATIONS
-------------------------------------------
Normal case — `medqa_347`:
The saved exam question goes through one full iteration. The Clarifier
structures the question, retrieval finds passages, candidate evidence is
compared with PubMed, and the final selector returns the benchmark answer with
a rationale. Confidence meets the threshold, so no second cycle is needed.
Original answer choices are visible because this is a benchmark question.

Adaptive case — `medqa_107`:
The first cycle has a low heuristic score, so the Optimizer suggests a more
focused search. The next cycle retrieves and compares evidence using the
refined query; its score meets the threshold, so the loop stops. This shows the
feedback mechanism, not proof that the second answer is medically correct in
general.

Live free-text case:
The user types symptoms without selecting a disease or answer choice. The
system structures them, searches its index and PubMed, and produces a generated
response plus supporting material. In the nosebleed/fever example, “epistaxis”
is a symptom-level RAG candidate; the final text recommends prompt clinical
evaluation. It does not diagnose the cause.


PAPER VS. OUR PROJECT — THE HONEST COMPARISON
--------------------------------------------
Paper elements reflected in our design:
  • Five named functional roles and an adaptive feedback loop.
  • BioBERT-style semantic retrieval and Qdrant vector search.
  • Confidence threshold 0.65 and maximum of three cycles.
  • A web prototype concept with free-text input and an annotated result.

Documented differences/limitations:
  • One Gemini model fills all five prompt roles; paper uses multiple models.
  • Explicit Python orchestration replaces CrewAI.
  • We use a local sample of MedRAG textbooks plus PubMed abstracts rather than
    the paper’s exact full corpus and wider web-search sources.
  • SNOMED CT/ICD-10 coding is not implemented.
  • The confidence formula is ours; the paper does not publish the formula.
  • Only 20 MedQA questions were evaluated; PubMedQA and MedBullets were not.
  • No clinical validation has been done. This is not for patient care.


PRESENTATION WORDING TO KEEP STRAIGHT
------------------------------------
Say: “candidate hypothesis,” “retrieved passage,” “PubMed abstract,”
“experimental heuristic,” “preliminary benchmark result,” and “prototype.”

Avoid saying: “the system diagnosed the patient,” “83% certain,” “clinically
validated,” “the paper’s exact formula,” “five independent AI models,” or “the
same full database as the paper.”
